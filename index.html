How to read this dashboard

The bar
52.026.022.0 The model's probability split for the match result. Blue is a home win, grey a draw, amber an away win. The three always add up to 100%. Figures are hidden on slivers too narrow to hold them – the full numbers are in the Result panel below each bar.
White ticks
Where the bookmaker splits the same match, after their margin is removed. When a tick sits on a colour boundary, model and bookmaker agree. The further a tick sits from its boundary, the more they disagree. No ticks means that fixture isn't priced yet.
Percentages
The model's probability for each outcome. Read them as frequencies: an outcome shown at 30% should happen roughly three times in ten, so it losing seven times out of ten is the model working, not failing.
@ prices
@2.50 is the bookmaker's decimal price. It's shown as published, margin included – so it isn't directly comparable with the percentage beside it. The edge figure does that comparison for you.
Edge figures
+7.2 -6.1 +1.4
Model probability minus the bookmaker's margin-adjusted probability, in percentage points. Green means the model rates that outcome more likely than the bookmaker does; red less likely; grey a gap too small to mean anything.
Confidence
HIGH both teams have plenty of matches behind their ratings.
MEDIUM a moderate amount – treat with some caution.
LOW at least one team has very few matches. Common in August and after promotion. Percentages are still shown, but they rest on thin evidence.
Book margin
The bookmaker's built-in edge on these markets, averaged. Their raw prices imply more than 100% in total; the surplus is the margin. It's stripped out before any comparison here, which is why the edge figures are meaningful rather than uniformly positive.
Track record
How the model has actually done on matches it predicted before they were played.

Calibration is the headline because it's the honest test: of everything called 60%, did roughly 60% happen? Hit rate is shown too, but read it against the always-pick-home baseline beside it – on its own it flatters a model that just backs favourites. Skill below zero means the model is doing worse than simply knowing how often home teams win.
Missing players
Who is injured or suspended, per side. Highlighted names are players the model's data says are near ever-present for that club – at least 70% of the minutes of their most-used team-mate last season.

These names do not change the percentages above. The model works on team-level attack and defense ratings and has no concept of individual players. This panel is here so you can apply the judgement it can't.
Priced
How many of the listed fixtures have odds attached. Bookmakers price only a short window ahead, so matches further out legitimately show dashes.
What a big edge does and doesn't tell you. A large gap means the model and the bookmaker disagree. It does not establish which of them is right, and it is not a signal to act on. Bookmaker prices carry team news, injuries, lineups, suspensions and the weight of professional money – none of which this model can see. It knows goals scored and conceded, and nothing else. So when a simple goals-based model disagrees sharply with the market, the likeliest explanation by far is that the market knows something the model doesn't.

In practice the most useful thing to do with a very large edge is treat it as a data problem to investigate rather than a discovery: check the confidence flag, check whether a team was recently promoted, check the name matched correctly. The honest use of these columns is measuring whether your model is well calibrated over a few hundred matches – not picking out individual fixtures.
